## 1. The through-line

> Understand before you solve. Diagnose before you respond. Make the customer the centre of the message.

Most customer messages fail not because the facts are wrong but because:
- the customer never felt understood **before** they were given a solution or a boundary, or
- the message answers the *keyword* instead of the *reason it mattered*, or
- the tone did not match how the customer prefers to engage.

The three-channel output (Email / Call Script / Voicemail) exists precisely so the same understanding
reaches the customer through whichever channel they actually use — so all three must carry identical
facts and identical intent.

## 2. Diagnose before you write

### 2.1 Situation type
Service recovery · objection handling · refund/return/exchange · dispute · escalation ·
informational reply · proactive outreach. The type sets the default tone and which objections to pre-load.

### 2.2 Competitive vs collaborative (Michigan + Coursera M5)
Neither style is better; effectiveness depends on **fit**. Choose deliberately with three diagnostic factors:

| Factor | Question |
|---|---|
| **Relationship duration** | One-time transaction or ongoing relationship? |
| **Strategic importance** | Does their outcome affect revenue, operations, or reputation? |
| **Interdependence and risk** | How costly is conflict to each side? |

- **Competitive** — claim value. For transactional, low-switching-cost, low-relationship-risk cases. Be clear and firm on boundaries.
- **Collaborative** — create value before dividing it. For strategic/ongoing relationships. Uncover interests before defending a position.

The two classic mistakes: defaulting to competitive everywhere; over-collaborating where clear
boundaries are required. **Most breakdowns happen when people default to a single style.**

### 2.3 Positions vs interests
- **Position** = what the customer asked for ("I want 15% off").
- **Interest** = why they asked (budget pressure, being made whole, predictability, being respected).
- When you hit a standoff, move from positions to interests. Script: *"What internal constraints are shaping this request?"*

### 2.4 BATNA, reservation price, ZOPA (Michigan)
| Concept | Definition | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **BATNA** | Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement — what the customer will do if you say no (leave, escalate, review publicly, churn) | If the deal is worse than your BATNA you walk; theirs yours to anticipate |
| **Reservation price** | Your hard boundary (max refund, minimum notice, deadline) | Never cross without approval |
| **ZOPA** | Zone of Possible Agreement between the two reservation prices | If it exists, there is room for a deal |

Strong BATNA on the customer side (high churn value, public visibility) → lean collaborative.
Weak BATNA → a clear boundary is safe.

### 2.5 The 7 preparation questions (Michigan)
1. Should I even negotiate? 2. Positional or interest-based? 3. Dispute resolution or deal-making?
4. Analysis framework (goal, issues, why they matter, BATNA, reservation price, probable price, stretch goal).
5. What is my BATNA? 6. What is theirs (put yourself in their seat)? 7. Is there a ZOPA?

## 3. Listening, so the customer feels understood

### 3.1 The three core behaviours (Coursera M1)
1. **Paraphrase it** — restate in your own words and check: *"So the main concern is not cost, it's rollout risk. Did I get that right?"*
2. **Open-ended questioning** — *"How does timeline pressure show up for your team right now?"* (not *"Is timeline important?"*)
3. **Summarize** — what matters most, **why**, and the constraints. Concise, neutral, customer-centred. No interpretation, no solutions yet.

Buyers judge listening by **accuracy**, not politeness.

### 3.2 The three mistakes that break trust
Listening to respond (not understand) · jumping to solutions too fast · selective listening
(filtering for facts that confirm your solution).

### 3.3 Listen past the keyword (Coursera M2)
**Keywords are nouns; needs are meanings.** "Budget" can mean cost exposure, pressure to justify
spend, or fear of paying twice. Three deeper layers:
1. **Intent** — what are they trying to achieve or avoid?
2. **Priority** — what feels most urgent or emotionally charged?
3. **Concern / Risk** — what could go wrong if they decide wrong?

### 3.4 Five listening steps (CS)
Be ready to listen → ask the right questions (open to explore, closed to confirm) → take notes →
show you're listening → **paraphrase back** (and listen "around the edges" for emotion and tone).

### 3.5 Note discipline
Bullets, not paragraphs · customer language, not sales language · needs and concerns, not solutions.
Test: *Would a colleague who never heard the call act correctly? Would the customer recognise themselves?*

## 4. Rapport (Coursera M9–M10)

Rapport is **not** charisma or long small talk. It begins in the **first two minutes** and is built
through **alignment, not performance** — it adapts to cues rather than running a script.

**Three techniques — use ONE, then adapt:**
1. **Mirroring** — subtly match tone, pace, formality. Never imitate mannerisms; exaggerated mirroring destroys authenticity.
2. **Small-talk alignment** — briefly acknowledge their immediate context. *"I only have a few minutes." → "Thank you for letting me know; I will keep us focused and respect the time."*
3. **Shared experiences** — a brief, relevant professional reference that normalises their concern, keeping them at the centre.

**Opening = Structure + Connection.** Structure (who's leading, why we're meeting, that time is
respected) creates ease; connection (one well-timed behaviour) creates comfort.

**Mistakes:** overdoing small talk · forced/exaggerated mirroring · oversharing or assuming closeness ·
extending casual talk after the customer signals readiness to proceed. All fail because **effort
became visible.**

## 5. Buyer / communication styles (Coursera M11, DISC)

> Most buyers don't object to *what* you say. They react to *how* it is delivered.

| Style | Orientation | Email cue | Call cue | Adapt by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Driver (D)** | Results, speed, brevity | Short, fact-first sentences | Rapid, goal-oriented questions | Lead with the outcome; bullets; no filler |
| **Influence (I)** | Big picture, story, enthusiasm | Warm greeting, narrative | Big-picture commentary, story-weaving | Open warmly; brief change story; energetic close |
| **Amiable (S)** | People, reassurance, team | Warmth and gratitude | Reassurance / team-impact questions | Slow down; validate; emphasise continuity |
| **Analytical (C)** | Data, evidence, proof | Requests for data, links, attachments | Repeated clarification, data asks | Evidence first; cite the source; no unsupported claims |

**Do not label people to them.** Awareness of patterns, not taxonomy or a rigid script.

## 6. Message construction

### 6.1 Storytelling — Situation → Struggle → Solution (Coursera M3)
- *Slides inform; stories stick.* Stories don't replace facts, they give facts meaning.
- **Situation** (~25% of the story): who they were, the environment, the constraints.
- **Struggle** (the most important part): what wasn't working, why it mattered to the business. *If the struggle feels weak, the outcome feels unearned.*
- **Solution → outcomes**: what changed and what improved, with one or two specific results. **Never list features here.**
- Five steps: decide the point → write the situation briefly → make the struggle do the work → tie the solution to outcomes → edit for clarity.

### 6.2 Feature → Benefit → Outcome (Coursera M7)
| Level | Answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Feature | What it has/does | "Automated reporting" |
| Benefit | How it helps | "Saves your team time" |
| **Outcome** | Why it matters to the bottom line | "Reduces reporting time 40%, freeing 10 hrs/week for faster decisions" |

Executives reward **improved performance**, not the coolest product. Match the frame to the
stakeholder: CFO = cost / ROI, compliance = risk, operations = speed.

### 6.3 The four-part outcome value proposition (Coursera M8)
1. **Frame the business challenge** → 2. **State the measurable outcome** → 3. **Quantify the impact** (metric + baseline + source; a range with stated assumptions beats vagueness) → 4. **Align strategically** with what the business already tracks.

### 6.4 Collaborative negotiation, 5 steps (Coursera M6)
1. **Open with shared purpose** (*"Our goal is to find something that works for both sides."*)
2. **Surface interests** behind positions
3. **Ask diagnostic questions** (*"What outcomes matter most to you?"*)
4. **Explore trade-offs** — think in packages, not issue-by-issue
5. **Confirm mutual gains** — summarise what was agreed and the benefit to both sides

## 7. Service recovery — the hard half of customer communication

### 7.1 What customers expect (CS)
**CARE factors that create expectations:** Competition · Advertising · Reputation (word of mouth is
the strongest) · Experience.

**Six dimensions of service quality:** Reliability (the entry ticket) · Responsiveness ·
Feeling valued (not a number) · **Empathy** (the most lacking skill) · Competency ·
**Dealing with one person** (customers don't want to be passed around — they want one voice that owns the problem).

### 7.2 When a customer is angry — the 5-step strategy (CS)
1. **Stay calm** — use **STOP**: *Signal* (notice your own anger cues) → *Take control* → *Opposite* (do the opposite of the warning sign) → *Practice*.
2. **Let them vent** — listen without interrupting; use the "power of the pen" (take notes visible in the conversation).
3. **Handle the emotion first** — logic doesn't land until the emotion is set aside. Show empathy, find areas of agreement, reframe, thank them.
4. **Avoid trigger words** — swap "policy"→"here's what we can do", "can't"→"can", "no"→"sorry/I understand", "I don't know"→"I can find out", "but"→"and", "you should have"→"what others have found helpful", "the only thing we can do"→"a good option for you is".
5. **Delay action or get a second opinion** — *"I want to give you the best solution. Let me look into this and get back to you in a few minutes."*

> **Never deliver bad news for the first time in writing or in a voicemail.** Bad news is like a dead fish — it only gets worse with time, so deliver it early, in person or by phone, and get to the point ("You're not going to like hearing this").

### 7.3 Recovery skills (CS)
Thank them for coming to you · take responsibility (no blame, no excuses) · resolve quickly ·
**involve the customer in the solution** · do something extra to offset the "nuisance factor"
(**only within already-approved options — never a cash or compensation promise; if no approved
option exists, acknowledge the hassle in words instead**) · apologise at the **end**, not the start · follow up.

### 7.4 Delivering bad news (CS)
Tell them as early as possible · in person or by phone, not by letter/email · get to the point ·
treat them fairly (they will remember) · apologise sincerely · ask for another chance to serve them ·
don't let it affect your next customer.

## 8. Telephone skills — the four stages (CS) *(drives the Call Script channel)*

1. **Greeting** — the 3 Ps: **Pleasant, Professional, Positive**. Smile (they can hear it), speak clearly, use their name, check your own mood/energy, gauge and respond to theirs.
2. **Gathering** — be ready to listen and take notes, show you're listening (attentive silence + attentive words), ask questions, paraphrase.
3. **Responding** — answer/resolve immediately, put on hold to fetch an answer, or consult a leader. **Hold tips:** ask permission (*"May I put you on hold?"*), give a specific estimate, wait for the answer before pressing the button, never leave someone more than ~45 seconds without an update, say "thank you for waiting" every time you return.
4. **Renewal** — be personal, not programmed; avoid standard closings; ask if they need anything else; repeat your name; thank them for their business.

**Transferring calls:** say what you *can* do, not what you can't; avoid the word "transfer"
(*"let me connect you with Maria"*); brief your colleague first; never "throw" the customer to
someone's voicemail; never transfer more than once; if you can't help after two tries, take the
number and call back.

## 9. Negotiation psychology — traps to avoid (Michigan)

| Trap | Antidote |
|---|---|
| **Fixed-pie myth** (assuming interests are always in direct conflict) | Ask: is this really zero-sum? Are there different interests that expand the pie? |
| **Reactive devaluation** (devaluing a proposal just because the other side made it) | Judge the proposal on its **content**, not its author. |
| **Escalation** (committing harder to a losing course) | Set limits **before** you start; watch for the hidden "second-bidder pays" trap. |
| **Competitive arousal** (rivalry + time pressure → rushed decisions) | Limit the most intense person's involvement; manage time; share responsibility. |
| **Framing / prospect theory** (losses loom larger than gains) | Ask: *how is this being framed — as a gain or a loss?* State losses plainly rather than dressing them up. |
| **Heuristics** (mental shortcuts that exclude the best option) | Recognise they simplify but can mislead; check the Munger-style list before deciding. |

**The distinction that separates good from excellent:** *seeing the deal from the other side* —
calculating the other party's answers to the same analysis questions you asked yourself.

## 10. Master dos and don'ts

**Do**
- Make your understanding visible before you present a solution or a boundary.
- Confirm meaning, then advance.
- Quantify with metric + baseline + source.
- Give one next step, with an owner and a date.
- State what you *can* do before what you can't.
- Match tone to the channel and to the customer's style.

**Don't**
- Draft your reply while they're still talking.
- Jump to solutions before understanding lands.
- List features while the customer is objecting.
- Overpromise or imply an unapproved exception.
- Bury the key message mid-paragraph.
- Use trigger words, jargon, ALL CAPS, or fake urgency.
- Let the three channels drift apart.

## 11. Phrase bank (verbatim, reusable)

**Confirm understanding**
- "So it sounds like the main concern is not cost. It is rollout risk. Did I get that right?"

**Open questions**
- "How does timeline pressure show up for your team right now?"
- "What outcomes are most important to you in this agreement?"
- "What internal constraints are shaping this request?"
- "How will this decision be evaluated within your organization?"

**Rapport**
- "Thank you for letting me know; I will keep us focused and respect the time."

**Outcome framing**
- "Our platform reduces manual analysis time by 35%, saving approximately $150,000 annually and enabling faster strategic decision making within the first quarter."
- "For organizations experiencing reporting delays, our platform reduces manual reporting time by 30–40% within 6 months… We'll validate your baseline in discovery, but the measurable outcome is faster reporting and quicker executive decision cycles."

**Negotiation**
- "Our goal today is to structure an agreement that supports your growth plans while allowing us to deliver consistently."
- "We have agreed on a two-year extension with phased pricing and performance benchmarks. This gives your team predictable budgeting while helping us plan capacity and service delivery."
